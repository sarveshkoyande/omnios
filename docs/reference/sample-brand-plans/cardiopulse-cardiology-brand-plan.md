# CardioPulse -- Brand Plan

*This is a fictional, illustrative brand-plan document, written to exercise the kit-update
ingestion flow. Nothing in it describes a real product, a real clinical trial, or real
patient/physician data. It is not approved content and must never be treated as one.*

## Who CardioPulse is

CardioPulse doesn't talk like a company trying to close a sale. It talks like the
cardiologist you'd actually want reading your echo -- unhurried, precise, and quietly
sure of itself because it did the homework. It never raises its voice to make a point;
it just puts the number in front of you and lets the number do the work. If CardioPulse
walked into a tumor board -- sorry, a heart failure clinic -- it would be the person who
brought the actual slide deck instead of the leave-behind.

## The clinical story, in plain terms

CardioPulse is positioned for adults with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction
who are already on background therapy but still cycling through hospital admissions.
The pitch to a cardiologist is simple: fewer readmissions, a manageable tolerability
profile, and dosing that doesn't ask a patient to reorganize their life around a pill
schedule.

## HCP personas

### Dr. Priya Nandakumar -- "The Readmission Hawk"

Priya runs the heart failure clinic at a mid-sized regional hospital, seeing somewhere
around 35 HFrEF patients a week between clinic and inpatient consults. Her whole world
revolves around one number: 30-day readmission rate, because that's what her hospital
system measures her department on. She's seen enough "promising" therapies fail to
move that number that she's become almost allergic to marketing language -- if a rep
opens with "breakthrough," she's already mentally checked out. What actually gets her
attention is a real hospitalization-risk reduction number with a confidence interval
she can defend to her own chief of cardiology. She reads NEJM and Circulation cover to
cover most weeks and would rather get a two-page PDF of the actual trial data emailed
to her than sit through a fifteen-minute detail visit. Her biggest daily frustration
isn't the drugs themselves -- it's patients who feel better after two weeks and quietly
stop refilling, only to bounce back three months later. "I don't need another drug that
works in the trial," she says. "I need one that survives contact with a patient who
feels fine on a Tuesday and forgets by Thursday."

### Dr. Marcus Webb -- "The Access Realist"

Marcus is a general internist in a community practice who co-manages HFrEF patients
alongside a rotating cast of cardiology consults he doesn't always get to talk to
directly. He's pragmatic bordering on skeptical about anything that adds friction to
his day -- prior authorizations, complicated titration schedules, patient copays he
knows will tank adherence before the drug even gets a chance to work. He prefers reps
who show up with a one-pager on formulary status and copay assistance rather than a
deep dive into mechanism of action, and he's told more than one sales rep, half-joking,
"I don't care how it works, I care whether my patient can actually afford to keep
taking it." Once-daily dosing is a real selling point for him -- not because he doubts
the science, but because he's watched twice-daily regimens quietly fail in patients who
are already juggling five other medications.

## Brand voice notes

CardioPulse's tone pillars: evidence-first, unhurried, quietly confident, never
salesy. The brand avoids words like "breakthrough," "revolutionary," or "game-changing"
entirely -- if the data is strong, the number should be the headline, not the adjective.
Every efficacy claim in CardioPulse material is required to sit next to its safety
context on the same page, never on a follow-up slide. The brand's internal shorthand
for this discipline is "no claim travels alone."
